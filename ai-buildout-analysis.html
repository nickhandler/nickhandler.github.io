Will the AI Buildout Pay Off?

An investment analysis of the $700B+ annual infrastructure race

August 30, 2026

My view: AI succeeds. The buildout probably succeeds. But portions of the investment boom almost certainly get overbuilt.

The important part is separating two different questions that keep getting collapsed into one: will AI succeed, and will the AI capex cycle generate adequate returns. Those are not the same question, and most of the current debate suffers from treating them as if they were.

OutcomeProbabilityWhat it means
AI becomes transformative and most infrastructure earns attractive returns 55% Best-case structural AI boom
AI succeeds, but significant infrastructure is overbuilt 35% Technology wins; many investments don't
AI demand materially disappoints and buildout becomes a bust 10% True AI bubble scenario
You can be extremely bullish on AI while still believing the AI infrastructure cycle eventually experiences a painful correction.

The internet provides the obvious precedent. The internet transformed civilization, yet telecom companies massively overbuilt fiber during the dot-com era and investors lost enormous amounts of money. Something less extreme but conceptually similar is plausible with AI.

Today, however, the evidence still favors genuine demand rather than speculative construction without customers. NVIDIA's latest quarter produced $89 billion of Data Center revenue, up 117% YoY. Microsoft says it remains capacity constrained. Alphabet expects to remain supply constrained through 2026. Google Cloud backlog has reached $240 billion. The problem isn't demand today — the question is whether demand eventually becomes large enough to justify an infrastructure bill approaching historic proportions.

Section 1

Just how enormous has the AI buildout become?

This is where the debate starts. J.P. Morgan currently estimates hyperscaler capital expenditures will reach approximately $697 billion in 2026. For perspective, annual investment from Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Oracle and Nvidia had roughly tripled from about $150 billion in 2023 to potentially more than $500 billion in 2026.

Individual companies are now talking about astonishing numbers on their own: Alphabet expects $175–185B of 2026 capex, Meta expects $130–145B, and Microsoft spent $41B in just its latest quarter. This month NVIDIA announced partnerships with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR designed to mobilize more than $500 billion of third-party capital over time for AI infrastructure.

We're no longer talking simply about tech companies buying GPUs. We're talking about an entire capital ecosystem: data centers → semiconductors → networking → electricity generation → transmission → cooling → land → construction → private credit → investment-grade debt → project finance. AI has effectively become a major global infrastructure investment cycle.

SIMPLE VERSION

Companies are spending an absolutely ridiculous amount of money building the physical infrastructure behind AI — approaching $700 billion of hyperscaler capex this year alone. That's why investors are nervous. When companies spend this much money, AI can't merely become useful. It needs to become extremely valuable.

Section 2

The strongest argument that the buildout succeeds

There is one fact that makes this substantially more bullish: companies still don't appear able to build AI infrastructure fast enough. If we were seeing empty data centers, collapsing GPU utilization, hyperscalers canceling projects, cloud growth slowing, and AI startups cutting compute consumption, that would be extremely concerning. Instead, we're seeing essentially the opposite.

Microsoft's commercial remaining performance obligation reached $678 billion, up 84% YoY. Even excluding OpenAI, RPO increased 25%. Microsoft said all sequential commercial RPO growth came from customers outside frontier-model companies, and Microsoft Cloud revenue reached $59.3 billion, growing 27%, with nearly 90% of full-year cloud revenue coming from customers outside frontier-model companies. That matters: one bearish thesis has been that a handful of AI labs are basically recycling venture capital into Nvidia GPUs and cloud providers. Microsoft's numbers weaken that argument — the demand base is broadening.

Alphabet tells a similar story: Google Cloud backlog has reached $240 billion after more than doubling year over year, nearly 75% of Google Cloud customers have used Google's vertically optimized AI offerings, and management expects to remain supply constrained throughout 2026. Meanwhile NVIDIA just reported revenue of $96.2B (+106%), Data Center revenue of $89.0B (+117%), and next-quarter guidance of $108B ±2%.

SIMPLE VERSION

The bullish argument is basically: if this is already a giant bubble, why are customers still asking for more computing power than companies can provide? Microsoft and Google aren't sitting on unused AI infrastructure — they're still racing to add capacity. The customers are already showing up.

Section 3

But here's the strongest bearish argument

Revenue must eventually catch up with spending. J.P. Morgan Asset Management estimates that generating merely a 10% return on current AI investment could require roughly $650 billion of annual AI revenue. That's the number worth keeping in the back of your mind through this entire debate.

Infrastructure spending can generate enormous economic value while still producing mediocre investment returns. Suppose the industry collectively spends $5 trillion building AI infrastructure. If that infrastructure generates only $250 billion of sustainable incremental annual profit, that's a 5% ROI — not remotely the return investors expect for taking on this much technological and execution risk.

Goldman Sachs highlights another problem: consumer AI adoption has been spectacular, but many consumers are using free products. Enterprise monetization becomes crucial, and most companies outside the semiconductor portion of the AI ecosystem have yet to generate substantial profits directly attributable to AI. Usage is not the same thing as monetization. ChatGPT and Gemini can have hundreds of millions of users and AI can generate trillions of tokens — none of that by itself proves the infrastructure earns attractive returns. Someone ultimately has to pay enough for those tokens.

ROI = 250 / 5,000 = 5%
SIMPLE VERSION

Imagine building the greatest gym in New Jersey for $1 million, and it's packed every day — but almost everyone pays $5 a month. It's still a terrible investment. We already know people want AI. What we don't know yet is whether people and businesses will pay enough for it to justify the money being spent building it.

Section 4

The hidden variable: AI needs to create productivity

Ultimately the economics have to work their way down to businesses. If a company pays $10 million annually for AI and that AI helps it reduce labor costs, increase productivity, write software faster, or improve pricing and logistics enough to create $30 million of value, adoption becomes obvious.

McKinsey argues AI economics are already approaching viability for many use cases, but most companies still report relatively little measurable earnings impact because implementation remains difficult. The issue increasingly isn't the cost of the technology — it's reorganizing workflows and companies around it. This mirrors earlier general-purpose technologies: installing computers didn't instantly make companies dramatically more productive, and factories that replaced steam engines with electric motors without redesigning around electricity saw little gain until they reorganized production entirely.

SIMPLE VERSION

AI eventually needs to make companies more money than it costs them. If a bank spends $100 million on AI and saves or generates $500 million, AI becomes a no-brainer — and that's the chain that ultimately justifies today's buildout: banks want it, providers need more compute, hyperscalers build more data centers, NVIDIA sells more GPUs.

Section 5

Why inference may matter more than training

This is one of the biggest structural reasons for optimism. The first stage of AI was dominated by training — building GPT, Gemini, Claude, and training increasingly enormous models. That's extraordinarily compute intensive, but training happens periodically. Inference is different: it occurs every single time someone actually uses AI, whether that's a chat query, an agent analyzing thousands of documents, a self-driving vehicle interpreting its surroundings, or a robot navigating a warehouse.

AI compute demand could transition from "building intelligence" toward "using intelligence billions or trillions of times per day." McKinsey estimates global data-center demand could nearly triple from approximately 82 GW in 2025 to roughly 220 GW in 2030 under current adoption scenarios. If AI agents become ubiquitous, inference could ultimately dwarf today's training workloads.

SIMPLE VERSION

Training is building the car. Inference is every mile it ever drives. The bull case isn't just that companies keep building smarter AI — it's that billions of people and businesses eventually use those AIs constantly. That's potentially much bigger.

Section 6

The biggest threat: efficiency

Here's a fascinating paradox: AI models are becoming dramatically more efficient, which sounds bullish but can initially sound bearish for infrastructure investors. If answering a query today takes 100 units of compute and three years from now takes 10, you need 90% less compute per query — does that destroy demand?

Possibly, but there's an economic phenomenon called the Jevons paradox: when something becomes dramatically cheaper, people often consume vastly more of it. If AI inference becomes 90% cheaper but usage increases 100x, total compute demand still explodes. Whichever force wins — adoption growth or efficiency gains — determines infrastructure demand.

Compute Demand = AI Usage × Compute per Task
SIMPLE VERSION

Bear says: AI got 10x cheaper, so we need fewer GPUs. Bull says: no, now companies can afford to use AI for 100x more things. If usage grows faster than efficiency improves, we still need dramatically more computing power — that's the base case here.

Section 7

The depreciation problem is underrated

This is where the credit and fixed-income lens becomes particularly interesting. A traditional data-center building can remain useful for decades. A GPU cannot. Microsoft disclosed that roughly two-thirds of its latest quarterly capex was for shorter-lived assets — primarily CPUs and GPUs — while the remainder went toward longer-lived assets like data-center sites. Alphabet similarly says roughly 60% of its infrastructure investment goes toward servers, with about 40% toward data centers and networking.

Today's GPU investment isn't a one-time cost — Blackwell gives way to Rubin gives way to the next architecture, and equipment becomes economically obsolete. Alphabet's depreciation increased from $15.3 billion in 2024 to $21.1 billion in 2025, and expects that growth to accelerate materially in 2026. Hyperscalers need to generate enough cash not merely to justify today's investment but to continually refresh enormous amounts of hardware.

SIMPLE VERSION

A data-center building might last 40 years. The GPUs inside it absolutely won't — it's like spending billions on race cars knowing much faster ones are coming out every few years. Companies need to make their money back fast enough before today's hardware becomes outdated.

Section 8

Power may actually become the bottleneck

People often think the limiting factor is GPUs. Increasingly, it may be electricity. A large AI data center can require gigawatts of power, and you can't simply decide tomorrow to build another 5 GW — you need generation, transmission, transformers, interconnection, land, permits, cooling, and construction. Goldman Sachs is explicitly focusing on power demand, hyperscaler spending, and community resistance to data-center construction as major variables. J.P. Morgan similarly identifies power availability, permitting, and supply-chain constraints as factors that can materially delay projects.

Ironically, this constraint may protect returns. If infrastructure could be built instantly, everyone would build enormous capacity, supply could explode, and compute prices would collapse. Power bottlenecks restrict how quickly supply can enter the market.

SIMPLE VERSION

AI companies have basically unlimited ambition, but not unlimited electricity — and that slows down how quickly data centers can be built. That partially protects existing infrastructure by preventing everyone from flooding the market with capacity overnight.

Section 9

Why the Cisco comparison is imperfect

Cisco was one of the defining infrastructure beneficiaries of the internet boom: traffic exploded, telecoms built massive networks, Cisco sold the equipment, and its revenue exploded with it. Investors extrapolated that growth indefinitely — then telecom infrastructure became overbuilt, spending collapsed, competition intensified, and Cisco's valuation compressed dramatically.

The lesson isn't "Nvidia equals Cisco." The lesson is that even if the technology changes the world, infrastructure investment can temporarily outrun economically justified demand. NVIDIA differs from Cisco in one important way — it isn't merely selling interchangeable hardware, but an ecosystem of GPUs, CUDA, networking, systems and libraries, which broadens its competitive moat considerably. And AI computation may have a much larger recurring workload through inference. But NVIDIA still faces the same fundamental law Cisco faced: customers cannot indefinitely increase capex faster than the economic value that capex generates.

SIMPLE VERSION

Saying "Cisco crashed, therefore NVIDIA crashes" is too simplistic. The real lesson from Cisco is that technology can completely change the world while investors still overestimate how much infrastructure needs to be built at any one moment. That's the risk NVIDIA investors should actually care about.

Section 10

Why this isn't yet 1999

Today's biggest AI spenders — Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta — are some of the most profitable corporations ever created, and they aren't primarily financing their AI ambitions with speculative equity markets. They have enormous existing cash flows, which dramatically extends the runway.

Meta illustrates both sides of this dynamic well: it spent $31.1 billion on capex in Q2 alone and generated only $784 million of free cash flow that quarter, yet revenue simultaneously grew 28% YoY to $60.8 billion, and management still expects 2026 operating income to exceed 2025. AI spending is consuming enormous cash, but the underlying businesses remain exceptionally profitable — which means the cycle doesn't necessarily end just because external financing becomes expensive.

SIMPLE VERSION

A lot of dot-com companies were big dreams, investor money, and no profits. Today's biggest AI spenders already generate enormous cash flows — they can afford to be wrong for much longer. That's a major difference.

Section 11

Where I think the bubble actually is

AI itself isn't the bubble, and NVIDIA probably isn't either. The area worth watching most closely is speculative infrastructure financed under aggressive assumptions about future utilization — certain data-center developments, GPU-cloud companies, leveraged infrastructure projects, private-credit structures, power projects, and businesses whose economics depend on permanently elevated compute scarcity. The more debt enters the ecosystem, the more dangerous overbuilding becomes. J.P. Morgan already describes AI infrastructure as requiring increasingly sophisticated combinations of corporate debt, project debt, and equity financing.

SIMPLE VERSION

If AI overbuilds, Microsoft probably isn't the first thing that blows up. Watch the operators borrowing enormous amounts of money to build data centers on the assumption that AI demand stays insane forever — those are the players that can get destroyed if utilization falls.

Section 12

The circular-financing concern

Capital flows between chipmakers, hyperscalers, AI labs, private equity, private credit, data-center developers, and infrastructure investors are increasingly interconnected. NVIDIA's newly announced partnerships to mobilize more than $500 billion of third-party infrastructure capital show just how deeply capital markets are becoming intertwined with AI compute expansion. This doesn't mean demand is fake, but it makes the quality of demand increasingly important — there's a large difference between a profitable company paying for AI because it generates real savings, and a venture-funded AI startup spending on compute while losing money, even though both create identical cloud revenue in the short run.

SIMPLE VERSION

Don't just ask whether AI revenue is growing — ask who is paying for it, and where they got the money. Normal profitable companies funding AI spending from the value it creates is extremely bullish. AI companies increasingly funding each other's growth with borrowed or investor money is much more concerning.

Section 13

What would make me turn bearish?

A thesis isn't useful if nothing can change your mind. Conviction would drop materially if several of these appeared at once:

  1. Hyperscalers stop describing themselves as capacity constrained
  2. Cloud AI revenue materially decelerates despite enormous capacity additions
  3. GPU utilization falls materially
  4. AI inference prices collapse faster than usage grows
  5. Enterprise AI pilots fail to convert into large production deployments
  6. Microsoft, Google, or Amazon AI backlog growth slows sharply
  7. Hyperscaler capex keeps rising while incremental revenue growth stalls
  8. Data-center vacancy rises substantially
  9. AI infrastructure credit spreads widen materially
  10. Hyperscalers begin canceling or delaying major projects for demand reasons rather than power or supply constraints
  11. NVIDIA inventory and customer receivables rise substantially faster than revenue
  12. Enterprise executives begin saying AI isn't generating measurable ROI
SIMPLE VERSION

The warning sign isn't "Microsoft spent another $50 billion." It's "Microsoft spent another $50 billion, but Azure growth slowed, customers aren't using the new capacity, AI pricing is falling, and companies aren't getting productivity gains." That combination is when concern is warranted.

Section 14

What would make me even more bullish?

The biggest confirmation would have little to do with NVIDIA directly — it would be AI moving into ordinary corporate income statements: a bank citing a specific reduction in expenses, a retailer citing a specific margin improvement, a pharma company citing materially shorter drug-development timelines, software customers willingly paying materially more for AI products. Once regular companies are consistently reporting that AI spend is generating a multiple of its cost, the infrastructure buildout becomes far easier to justify.

AI → Productivity → Corporate Profits → AI Spending → Cloud Revenue → Data Centers → Semiconductors
Section 15

Base case, 2026–2030

  • 2026–27 · Infrastructure boom continues Demand remains extremely strong, hyperscaler capex stays elevated, NVIDIA continues benefiting, power becomes increasingly important, AI inference grows rapidly, and capital markets provide increasingly large financing.
  • 2027–28 · Monetization becomes the dominant question Investors stop rewarding "we're investing aggressively in AI" and start asking "show me the return." The gap between AI winners and losers becomes substantially clearer, and enterprise adoption matters more than model benchmarks.
  • 2028–30 · Shakeout AI infrastructure supply temporarily overshoots demand somewhere in the ecosystem. Compute prices fall, some projects become uneconomic, leveraged operators struggle, some AI companies fail, capex growth slows, and NVIDIA likely experiences at least one substantial cyclical downturn. Usage keeps increasing, and the industry consolidates around the strongest operators.
SIMPLE VERSION

The story probably goes: AI boom → enormous spending → real productivity → some overbuilding → painful shakeout → long-term AI dominance. Not "everything works perfectly forever," and not "AI was useless."

Section 16

Investment implications

This distinction matters enormously — there are really three separate bets being made at once, and they deserve different levels of conviction:

Bet #1 — AI adoptionVery high conviction
AI becomes embedded across knowledge work, software, advertising, research, robotics, and other areas.
Bet #2 — AI compute demandHigh conviction
Inference, agents, and increasingly sophisticated models keep aggregate compute demand growing despite efficiency gains.
Bet #3 — Current infrastructure earns extraordinary returnsModerate conviction
Capital is pouring in, competition will increase, compute costs will fall, hardware depreciates rapidly, power is expensive, and technology changes quickly enough that supply can eventually overshoot demand.
SIMPLE VERSION

Roughly 90% bullish on AI, 80% bullish on long-term compute demand, and much less certain that every dollar spent today turns into $1.20 tomorrow. That's the distinction to hold onto.

Section 17

The fixed income angle: how this shows up in credit

Everything above is really an equity-market framing of the debate. But the AI buildout is increasingly being funded with debt, and debt investors ask a different question than equity investors. An equity holder wants to know if AI infrastructure earns an attractive return. A credit investor mostly wants to know if the entity that issued the bond can service its obligations even in a mediocre outcome. Those are not the same bar, and the AI financing stack is stratified enough that the answer changes dramatically depending on where in the capital structure you sit.

At the top of that stack are the hyperscalers themselves. Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta are financing the bulk of their capex from operating cash flow and, where they do issue debt, doing so as high-grade borrowers with balance sheets that can absorb a bad AI outcome without missing a coupon. Spreads on this cohort have stayed tight through the buildout precisely because the market isn't underwriting the AI thesis when it prices this paper — it's underwriting Azure, Google Search, AWS and the Family of Apps, all of which throw off cash whether or not AI infrastructure ever earns its cost of capital. This is the safest place to have AI exposure in a portfolio, and also the place earning the least compensation for that exposure.

Below that sits a fast-growing layer of purpose-built data-center debt: project finance, asset-backed securitizations collateralized by leases to hyperscalers or AI labs, and increasingly private credit facilities arranged by the same asset managers NVIDIA has partnered with to mobilize third-party capital. This paper looks very different. Its debt service depends on a single tenant's lease payments continuing on schedule for the life of the facility, on the underlying hardware still being useful enough at year seven or eight to justify the lease renewal, and on the developer not having over-levered the project on the assumption that AI demand stays insane forever. J.P. Morgan's own framing — that AI infrastructure increasingly requires sophisticated combinations of corporate debt, project debt and equity financing layered together — is itself a signal that underwriting standards are getting more creative, which is usually a late-cycle tell rather than an early-cycle one.

The specific risk to watch is tenant concentration. A data center financed against a 15-year lease to a single hyperscaler is a very different credit than one financed against a lease to a venture-funded AI lab burning investor cash. Both can look identical on a spreadsheet today — same cap rate, same yield, same coupon — but only one of those tenants can obviously make the twelfth lease payment in a downturn. The circular-financing dynamic described earlier (NVIDIA-adjacent capital funding data centers that lease back to AI labs that pay NVIDIA-adjacent capital) is exactly the kind of structure where the underlying cash flow quality can look fine until it very suddenly doesn't.

There's also a depreciation-driven refinancing risk that's specific to fixed income and doesn't really show up in the equity debate. A 15-year piece of data-center project debt is implicitly betting that GPUs bought today are still generating revenue in year ten. If Blackwell-generation hardware is functionally obsolete in four or five years, the facility's economics at refinancing depend on a second and third round of capex that may or may not be available on the same terms the first round enjoyed. That's a duration mismatch — long-dated debt against a rapidly depreciating asset — that credit investors have seen before in other cycles and generally price poorly the first time around.

None of this means AI infrastructure credit should be avoided. It means the diligence questions are different from the equity diligence questions. The things worth underwriting are: who's the actual tenant and what's their credit quality independent of AI hype, how much of the facility's value is in the (rapidly depreciating) hardware versus the (durably valuable) shell and power interconnection, how levered is the specific project rather than the parent company, and whether covenants exist that would trigger before a rating agency downgrade rather than after one. Spread compression across the sector right now suggests the market isn't yet differentiating much between these buckets — which is itself the opportunity, and the risk, depending on which side of the trade you're on.

SIMPLE VERSION

Lending to Microsoft to help fund its AI buildout is about as safe as corporate credit gets — they'd be fine paying you back even if AI never earns its keep, because the rest of their business covers it. Lending to a standalone data-center project that only gets paid if one AI tenant keeps paying rent for fifteen years on hardware that's obsolete in five is a completely different bet, even though both might show up in a portfolio as "AI infrastructure debt." The spread you're getting paid should reflect that difference. Right now, it often doesn't — which means the real fixed income opportunity in this cycle isn't picking the winning GPU maker, it's figuring out which pieces of the debt stack are mispriced relative to who's actually on the hook if utilization disappoints.

Section 18

Final verdict — does the AI buildout succeed?

Yes — that's the highest-conviction outcome here. The evidence today says demand is real: Microsoft's cloud revenue and backlog are exploding, Google Cloud's backlog has more than doubled, both companies continue describing capacity constraints, and NVIDIA just grew Data Center revenue 117% YoY. This is well beyond a purely speculative technology demonstration.

But the harder stage is next. The first question was whether AI can work — increasingly, yes. The second was whether people will use it — again, increasingly yes. The question that actually determines the investment outcome is whether AI can generate enough economic value to justify hundreds of billions of dollars of annual infrastructure spending. That's not settled, which is why the AI-bubble argument shouldn't be dismissed — it should be refined.

AI is probably one of the most important technological shifts of our lifetime. The infrastructure supporting it will therefore become enormously valuable. But the combination of extraordinary capital availability, competitive pressure, and extrapolation of current growth almost guarantees that portions of the ecosystem will eventually overbuild. The technology can succeed spectacularly while some of the investments financing it fail spectacularly.
THE WHOLE REPORT IN 30 SECONDS

AI is going to work. The demand looks legitimate — Microsoft and Google still can't build capacity fast enough, cloud backlogs are enormous, and Nvidia's data-center business just more than doubled.

But everyone is spending insane amounts of money to meet that demand — approaching $700 billion of hyperscaler capex this year. So the real debate isn't "is AI fake?" It's "can AI eventually make businesses enough money to justify all this spending?"

Probably yes, overall. But companies will also build too much capacity, some leveraged data-center bets will get crushed, some AI companies will disappear, and Nvidia will eventually see a real cyclical downturn. So the base case isn't "AI bubble pops" — it's AI wins. Some AI investments don't. That's a big difference.
